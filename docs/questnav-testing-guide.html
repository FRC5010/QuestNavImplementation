QuestNav Test Guide

How to set up the test area, measure the robot with the laser measure, calibrate the headset, and run the three test routines that measure how accurate and repeatable QuestNav is.

Overview

The tests use an AprilTag stand that stands in for the tags on the 2026 field. When the headset reads the tags, it aligns its pose to field coordinates.

Important

The QuestNav beta documents that it needs 2 tags in view at the same time to align to the field, and 3 for later corrections. With only tag 25, the headset possibly never aligns. Read One tag is possibly not enough before you trust a tag-based result.

Do the work in this order:

  1. How QuestNav works: background reading.
  2. Floor setup: one time for each test area.
  3. Laser measure: how to measure the robot at a mark.
  4. Offset calibration: find where the headset is on the robot.
  5. Test routines: measure accuracy, drift and repeatability.

0How QuestNav works

Read this section before you do the tests. It explains what the headset measures, why it drifts, and how AprilTags fix that. You do not need to know any of it already. Words in bold are terms that the rest of this guide uses.

The appendix has the same ideas with more math, for students who want it.

What "pose" means

To drive an auto, the robot must know where it is on the field. Three numbers describe that:

  • x: how far the robot is along the length of the field, in meters.
  • y: how far the robot is across the width of the field, in meters.
  • θ (theta), the heading: which way the front of the robot points, in degrees.

Together these three numbers are the robot's pose. A pose only means something if you know where the zero point is and which way the axes point. That set of rules is a coordinate frame. This guide uses the WPILib 2027 field frame in Figure B1:

  • The origin (0, 0) is the center of the field, where the long centerline crosses the midfield line.
  • +X points toward the blue alliance wall. The blue half of the field has positive x. The red half has negative x.
  • +Y points 90° counter-clockwise from +X, seen from above. For a driver at the blue wall, that is to the right.
  • A heading of 0° points toward the blue wall. Heading increases counter-clockwise.

Our code and the dashboard still use the 2026 frame. The robot code is on WPILib 2026, and the QuestNav beta uses the 2026 field layout. In the 2026 frame, the origin is the blue alliance corner, +X points toward the red wall, and a heading of 0° points toward the red wall. Every pose on the dashboard, in the snapshots and in the .wpilog is a 2026 pose until the team moves to WPILib 2027.

To change a 2026 pose to a 2027 pose (field 16.541 m × 8.069 m):

x2027 = 8.271 − x2026
y2027 = 4.035 − y2026
θ2027 = θ2026 + 180°

Example: tag 25 is at (4.02, 4.39), facing 180°, in the 2026 frame. In the 2027 frame it is at (4.25, −0.36), facing 0°. The same equations change a 2027 pose back to a 2026 pose. Distances, the tag-frame values and the test results do not change, because they do not depend on the field origin.

Part 1: Tracking with only the headset's own sensors

A Meta Quest headset was made for virtual reality. To draw a game world that does not swim when you move your head, it must know exactly how your head moves. QuestNav uses the same tracking to follow the robot. The headset has two kinds of sensors for this.

1. The IMU (inertial measurement unit). It has two parts:

  • A gyroscope measures how fast the headset turns.
  • An accelerometer measures how fast the speed changes (acceleration).

The IMU is very fast: it gives hundreds of readings each second. But it cannot measure position directly. To get position from acceleration, the software adds up the acceleration to get speed, and then adds up the speed to get position. Each small error in the acceleration is added up two times, so the position error grows very quickly. A tiny error of 0.01 m/s² becomes 0.5 m of position error after only 10 seconds. An IMU alone is useless for position after a few seconds.

2. The tracking cameras. The headset has several wide-angle cameras that look at the room. The software finds features in each picture: corners, edges and spots of texture, like a locker vent or a pattern in the carpet. When the headset moves, the features move in the picture. Near features move more than far features (Figure B2). From how the features move, the software calculates how the headset moved.

Together: visual-inertial odometry (VIO). "Odometry" means measuring how far you have moved. The headset combines the two sensors because each one covers the other's weakness:

IMUCameras
SpeedVery fastSlower
Good atQuick, sudden motionStaying correct over time
Bad atDrifts in secondsBlurry pictures, blank walls, the dark

The result is a smooth, accurate pose, 120 times each second. Think of walking down a hallway. With your eyes closed you can still feel your steps (the IMU), but after 20 steps you are not sure where you are. With your eyes open, you can see the lockers go past (the cameras), and you stay on track.

Where is zero?

VIO measures movement. It does not know where it is on the field. When tracking starts, the headset uses the point where it is at that moment as its zero point, and the direction it faces as its axes. This is the VIO frame. The QuestNav docs say it plainly: the origin is wherever the headset was when tracking started, and it is not related to the field.

So the same spot in the room can have completely different numbers, depending on where the headset woke up (Figure B3).

Before the robot can use the headset for an auto, something must connect the VIO frame to the field frame. Without AprilTags, the only way is to tell the robot where it starts: put the robot on a known spot (for example, against the alliance wall at a marked position) and reset the pose to that spot. After that, VIO measures all movement from there.

In our code
  • drivetrain.resetPose(...) sets the drivetrain pose. BLine autos call it at the start of a path (.withPoseReset(...) in RobotContainer).
  • QuestNavSubsystem.softReset(...) remembers an offset in code instead of sending a reset to the headset. Only the offset calibration uses it now.
  • The QuestNav library also has questNav.setPose(...), which sends a reset to the headset.

Drift

VIO is very good, but it is not perfect. Each small step has a tiny error, and the errors add up. This slow growth of error is drift.

Heading errors are the worst kind. If the heading is wrong by only 1°, every meter you drive is in a slightly wrong direction. After 5 m of driving, you are about 9 cm to the side of where you think you are. After a full match, the errors can be much larger.

Some things make the tracking worse:

  • Few features: blank walls, a bare floor, or a dark room.
  • Repeating patterns: a row of identical lockers can look the same from different places.
  • Things that move: people walking past, other robots. The software can think that the room moved.
  • Fast motion: sudden spins and hits blur the pictures.
  • Covered cameras: if the cameras cannot see, tracking stops. The dashboard shows QuestNav/Tracking as false, and QuestNav/TrackingLostCount goes up.

From the headset to the robot

The headset tracks itself, not the robot. The headset is on the side of the robot, not at the center. When the robot spins in place, the center stays still, but the headset moves in a circle. To get the robot's pose, the code needs to know exactly where the headset is on the robot and which way it faces. That is the mount transform.

A small mistake in the mount transform makes a large error when the robot turns. If the headset is 0.30 m from the center and the robot turns 90°, the headset moves 0.42 m. If the code thinks the headset is at the center, it thinks that the robot moved 0.42 m. This is why the offset calibration is the first test.

In our code
  • ROBOT_TO_QUEST in QuestNavSubsystem is the mount transform: x, y, z in meters and the rotation of the headset.
  • periodic() calculates robotPose = questPose.transformBy(ROBOT_TO_QUEST.inverse()): start at the headset, then move back to the robot center.

How the robot uses the headset

The robot does not use the headset alone. It also has wheel encoders and a gyro. A pose estimator combines all of them. Each source has a standard deviation: a number that says how much to trust it. A small number means "trust this a lot".

In our code
  • drivetrain.addVisionMeasurement(pose, timestamp, QUESTNAV_STD_DEVS) gives each headset pose to the estimator.
  • QUESTNAV_STD_DEVS is 0.02 m for x and y and about 5° for heading. 0.02 m is very small, so the robot trusts the headset position much more than the wheels.
  • The timestamp tells the estimator when the headset measured the pose, so a late message is applied at the correct time.

Part 2: Adding AprilTags

An AprilTag is a black and white square code, a little like a QR code, but simpler and easier to read from far away. FRC uses the 36h11 family. Each tag has an ID number. The field layout file lists the exact position, height and facing direction of every tag on the field. The 2026 tags are 16.51 cm (6.5 in) wide.

This changes everything. A tag is a known location. If the headset can see a tag and calculate where it is relative to the tag, it knows where it is on the field. The origin problem goes away.

How one picture gives a position

The software knows where the four corners of each tag are in the field. It finds the four corners in the camera picture. Then it calculates the one camera position and direction that makes the real corners land at those places in the picture. This calculation is called PnP (perspective-n-point).

The idea is simple:

  • A tag that looks big is close. A tag that looks small is far away.
  • A tag that looks squashed is seen from an angle. The shape tells the software the angle.
  • Where the tag is in the picture (left, right, up, down) tells the software which way the camera points.

Far tags give worse answers. At 5 m, the whole tag is only a few dozen pixels wide. If a corner is found one pixel wrong, the distance can be wrong by many centimeters. More tags in the same picture help, because the errors partly cancel. QuestNav uses this: it trusts a measurement less when the tags are far away and more when it sees several tags.

How QuestNav anchors to the field

QuestNav does not throw away VIO when it sees tags. VIO is still the main source: it is smooth and fast (120 times each second). Tags (about 30 times each second) correct it. The headset combines the two with a Kalman filter: a method that keeps a best guess and a "how sure am I" number, and blends in each new measurement by how much it trusts it.

The QuestNav docs describe two phases:

  1. Phase 1: alignment (one time). The first good tag view sets the field frame. QuestNav jumps the position to the tag answer and calculates a heading offset: the angle between the VIO frame and the field frame. From then on, the VIO movement is turned by that angle so that it is in field coordinates. The heading offset is then locked.
  2. Phase 2: corrections (all the time). Each later tag view that passes the checks gently pulls the position toward the tag answer. Close, multi-tag views pull hard. Far, single views pull a little. Heading is not corrected again.

The checks. A tag view is only used if it passes these checks. These are the values in the QuestNav docs for the 2026-3.0.0-beta release:

CheckPhase 1: alignmentPhase 2: corrections
Tags in the same picture2 or more3 or more
Inlier ratio (how many corners agree with the answer)60% or more80% or more
Jump from the current positionAnyLess than 2.0 m
What it changesPosition and headingPosition only
Important: one tag is possibly not enough

By these documented rules, a view with only one tag fails both phases. With only tag 25 on the stand, the headset possibly never aligns to the field, and its pose stays in the VIO frame. Then questnav/Distance To Target Tag is a number in the wrong frame, even when it looks reasonable.

Do one of these before you trust any tag-based result:

  • Add tag 26 to the stand. On the real field it is on the same hub face as tag 25: same height (1.124 m), same direction, 0.356 m (14 in) to the right of tag 25 as you face the tags. That gives 2 tags for alignment.
  • Check the QuestNav web UI for tag settings. The code changes for the beta mention confidence presets and a "Minimum Tags" setting, including a debug preset for single-tag tests on the bench. We could not confirm which of these are in the build on your headset.

Phase 2 corrections need 3 tags by default. With 2 tags, the Drift Loop shows the alignment but no later corrections.

Late pictures: rewind and replay

Finding tags in a picture takes time. When the answer is ready, the headset has already moved on. If the filter used the answer as if it were new, it would pull the pose to an old place. So QuestNav keeps the last half second of history. It rewinds to the moment the picture was taken, applies the correction there, and then replays all the VIO movement since then. Our robot code does the same thing on the robot side: that is why addVisionMeasurement takes a timestamp.

Safety and equipment

Warning

In Test mode and during calibration the robot moves without driver input. Keep people out of the test area. To stop the robot, disable it on the Driver Station.

Caution

The laser measure is a Class 2 laser. Do not look into the beam. Do not point it at people.

Equipment

  • The tag stand with tag 25
  • Bosch GLM100-23 laser measure
  • Target board: a straight, flat board about 2 m (6 ft) long, for example a 1×4 or a strip of plywood
  • White tape or white sticky notes, for the bumper corners
  • Steel tape measure
  • Carpenter square
  • Plumb bob (a string with a weight)
  • Floor tape in a bright color
  • Driver Station, controller, and a laptop with the dashboard

1Floor setup

Do this procedure one time for each test area. All measurements start from the floor mark below the tag.

  1. Add tag 26 (recommended). Mount it in the same plane as tag 25, at the same height, with its center 0.356 m (14 in) to the right of the tag 25 center as you face the tags. This matches the real hub face and gives the headset 2 tags for alignment.
  2. Check the tags. Each tag center must be 1.124 m above the floor. Hold a carpenter square against the tag face and the floor to make sure that the tag is vertical. If it is not correct, adjust the stand.
  3. Mark the point below the tag.
    1. Hang the plumb bob from the center of the tag.
    2. Let the plumb bob become stable.
    3. Put a mark on the floor where the plumb bob touches.
    Use this mark as the reference, not the base of the stand. Then the distance between the tag and the board does not have an effect on your measurements.
  4. Put a tag plane line on the floor. Run a tape line through the floor mark, parallel to the tag face. You measure distances out from this line.
  5. Put a centerline on the floor.
    1. Run a tape line from the floor mark, straight out from the tag face, for 5 m.
    2. Make sure that the centerline is square to the tag plane line. Use the 3-4-5 triangle (Figure 3).
  6. Put the target board on the tag plane line. Stand the board on its edge. Put its front face exactly on the tag plane line. If the base of the stand is in the way, use one board on each side of the stand. The board must reach about 1 m to each side of the centerline.
  7. Put cross lines on the centerline at 1 m, 2 m, 3 m and 4 m from the board face. These lines are only visual guides. Measure the exact values for each test.
  8. Tape the feet of the stand and the board to the floor.
Caution

If the stand or the board moves, all measurements after that are incorrect. If someone hits them, do the floor setup again.

2Laser measure

The Bosch GLM100-23 measures the distance from its rear edge to the point where the red dot lands. It is accurate to ±1.5 mm (1/16 in). Use it for the two long measurements, A and B. Use the tape for the short one, C.

Prepare the meter

  1. Set the units to meters, so that the readings agree with the dashboard. See the meter manual.
  2. Make sure that the meter measures from its rear edge. This is the default. The display shows the reference edge.
  3. Put a piece of white tape on each corner of the bumper side that faces the tag. The red dot is hard to see on a red bumper.

Why the meter goes on the board

Put the flat rear edge of the meter against the target board. Then the beam goes straight out from the tag plane, and the reading is the square distance to the tag plane. Do not hold the meter on the bumper. If the robot is at an angle, a beam from the bumper is not square to the tag, and the reading is too long.

Measure the robot at a mark

Measure three values on the bumper side that faces the tag. Routine 3 tells you which side faces the tag, and gives the values it expects.

  1. A, left corner:
    1. Put the rear edge of the meter flat against the target board.
    2. Turn on the laser.
    3. Slide the meter along the board until the red dot is on the white tape at the left corner, on the face of the bumper.
    4. Push the measure button. Record the reading.
  2. B, right corner: Do the same steps at the right corner.
  3. C, lateral: Find the middle of the facing bumper side. Measure with the tape from the centerline tape to that point, square to the centerline. Write a plus sign if the middle is to the right of the centerline, and a minus sign if it is to the left.
  4. Write the values on the record sheet next to the values from Test/Last Snapshot.
Note

Measure each corner two times. If the two readings are more than 2 mm apart, measure a third time and use the middle value.

A and B also show the heading. If A and B are equal, the bumper is square to the tag. A difference of 1 cm across a 0.88 m bumper is about 0.65°.

3Offset calibration

This procedure finds where the headset is on the robot. The result goes into ROBOT_TO_QUEST in QuestNavSubsystem.java. Do it before the test routines, and again if the headset mount moves.

Before you start

  • Look at the headset on the robot. Make sure that the yaw in ROBOT_TO_QUEST agrees with the direction the headset faces. The code now uses −90°: the headset faces the right side of the robot. The calibration does not find yaw. If the yaw is wrong, the result is rotated by the same angle.
  • Do a yaw check. Drive the robot forward 2 m along the centerline tape. The heading of the Quest robot pose must agree with the direction of travel.
  • Write down the current x and y values of ROBOT_TO_QUEST.
  • Find an open area with a minimum of 1.5 m of clear floor around the robot.
  • Cover the tag, or turn the stand away from the robot. If the headset sees a tag during a spin, the pose can jump and give a bad sample.
  • Turn on the headset and let it track for 1 minute.
  • Find questnav/Quest Calculated Offset to Robot Center on the dashboard. The value is [x, y] in meters, +X forward and +Y left.

Procedure

  1. Put the robot in the open area.
  2. Enable the robot in teleop.
  3. Push and hold the right bumper on the controller. The robot spins in short steps.
  4. Hold the bumper for a minimum of 10 cycles (about 30 seconds).
  5. Release the bumper. The robot stops.
  6. Record the [x, y] value.
  7. Turn the robot to a new start heading.
  8. Do steps 3 to 7 again until you have 3 runs.
Note

Each push of the bumper starts a new average. Do not release the bumper during a run.

Calculate and verify

  1. Compare the 3 runs. If they are not within about 1 cm of each other, look for wheel slip and do the runs again.
  2. Calculate the average x and the average y of the 3 runs.
  3. New x = current x + average x. New y = current y + average y.
  4. Put the new x and y into ROBOT_TO_QUEST. Do not change z or the rotation.
  5. Deploy the code and do one more run. The result must be about [0, 0], within about 1 cm.
  6. Measure from the robot center to the headset with the tape. The result must agree to within a few centimeters. The tracking point is inside the headset, so a tape cannot give an exact value.
Caution

The calibration sets the Quest pose to zero, so the pose is not in field coordinates after a run. Restart the robot code before you use the tag distance or the test routines.

4Test routines

The routines run in Test mode. Each routine drives the robot the same way every time and records labeled snapshots.

Before each session

  1. Turn on the headset and let it track for 1 minute.
  2. Put the robot 1.5 m to 2 m in front of the tag.
  3. Let the headset see the tags. With the default QuestNav settings it must see 2 tags at the same time to align to the field (why).
  4. Make sure that questnav/Distance To Target Tag agrees approximately with the tape.
  5. Measure the outside length and width of the robot, with bumpers. Set Test/Settings/Bumper Length (m) and Test/Settings/Bumper Width (m).

Run a routine

  1. Select the routine in the Test Routine chooser on the dashboard.
  2. Select Test mode on the Driver Station.
  3. Enable the robot. The routine starts.
  4. Watch Test/Status for the current step and the result.
  5. When the routine is done, disable the robot.

To run a routine again, disable and enable the robot again. Each run gets the next run number. The routines limit the speed to Max Speed (mps), 0.75 m/s by default. A routine stops if the next drive target is more than 6 m away. This usually means that the robot pose is not in field coordinates.

Routine 1: Turn Sweep

Results:

  • Test/Result/TurnSweep Max Shift (m): the largest change of the Quest position from the first snapshot.
  • Test/Result/TurnSweep Max Heading Error (deg): the largest difference between the Quest heading change and the gyro heading change.

How to read the results: If the shift increases with the angle, ROBOT_TO_QUEST is not correct. Do the offset calibration again. If the shift changes only at large angles, the headset possibly cannot see the tag at those angles.

Routine 2: Drift Loop

  1. The robot turns so that the headset points away from the tag.
  2. The robot drives the square for Drift Seconds. The headset continues to point away from the tag.
  3. The robot goes back to the start point and records the before tag snapshot.
  4. The robot turns to the tag and waits Tag View Seconds.
  5. The robot turns away again and records the after tag snapshot.

Results: Test/Result/Drift Correction (m) and Test/Result/Drift Heading Correction (deg) (the heading is compared with the gyro). Run it with Drift Seconds at 30, 60 and 150 (the length of a match). Keep Drift Square (m) smaller than the hallway.

Routine 3: Stop At Marks

At each mark:

  1. Wait until Test/Status says "measure".
  2. Read Test/Last Snapshot. It gives the bumper side that faces the tag and the values it expects for A, B and C.
  3. Measure A, B and C with the laser measure and the tape (Figure 4).
  4. Write the measured values next to the expected values.
  5. Press Y on the controller, or set Test/Continue to true. The robot drives to the next mark.

By default the headset points at the tag, so the right bumper faces the tag. At marks 5 and 6 the robot is at an angle, so A and B are different. To make the front bumper face the tag, set Test/Settings/Marks: Front Faces Tag to true. Then the headset cannot see the tag.

AAppendix: the math

This appendix is optional. It shows the math behind How QuestNav works, with worked numbers. You need algebra and some trigonometry (sine and cosine). Angles are in radians in the formulas: 1° ≈ 0.01745 rad.

A.1 Frames and transforms

A point on the robot, for example the headset, has a position (a, b) in the robot frame: a meters forward, b meters to the left of the robot center. If the robot pose is (x, y, θ), the same point in the field frame is:

xfield = x + a·cos θ − b·sin θ
yfield = y + a·sin θ + b·cos θ

First you turn the point by the robot heading, then you add the robot position.

Worked example. The robot is at (2.0, 1.0) with θ = 90° (facing +Y). The headset is on the right side: (a, b) = (0, −0.30).
xfield = 2.0 + 0·0 − (−0.30)·1 = 2.30
yfield = 1.0 + 0·1 + (−0.30)·0 = 1.0
The robot faces +Y, so its right side points toward +X. The headset is 0.30 m in +X from the center. Correct.

The code goes the other way: it knows the headset pose and wants the robot pose. That is the inverse transform: undo the mount offset. In WPILib this is one line: questPose.transformBy(ROBOT_TO_QUEST.inverse()).

A.2 How errors grow

IMU bias. If the accelerometer reads a constant error b (m/s²), adding it up two times gives a position error:

e(t) = ½ · b · t²

Heading error. If the heading is wrong by an angle ε, driving a distance d puts you to the side by:

e = d · sin ε ≈ d · ε   (ε in radians)
IMU bias 0.01 m/s²ErrorHeading error 1°Error
after 1 s0.005 mafter 1 m0.017 m
after 5 s0.125 mafter 5 m0.087 m
after 10 s0.50 mafter 15 m0.26 m

The IMU error grows with time squared, so the cameras must correct it all the time. The heading error grows with distance driven, so it matters most in long autos.

A.3 The offset calibration

Let d be the vector from the robot center to the point that the code tracks. When the robot turns in place by θ, the center does not move, but the tracked point moves by:

Δ = (R(θ) − I) · d    where   R(θ) = [ cos θ   −sin θ ;   sin θ   cos θ ]

R(θ) is the rotation matrix and I is the identity matrix. The code measures Δ and θ from the headset, then solves for d:

dx = [ (cos θ − 1)·Δx + sin θ·Δy ] / [ 2(1 − cos θ) ]
dy = [ −sin θ·Δx + (cos θ − 1)·Δy ] / [ 2(1 − cos θ) ]

Worked example. The headset is really at d = (0, −0.30), but ROBOT_TO_QUEST says (0, 0). The robot turns θ = 90°: cos θ = 0, sin θ = 1.
Δ = (R − I)·d = ( −1·0 − 1·(−0.30), 1·0 − 1·(−0.30) ) = (0.30, 0.30). That is 0.42 m of apparent movement.
Solve: dx = [ −1·0.30 + 1·0.30 ] / 2 = 0,   dy = [ −1·0.30 − 1·0.30 ] / 2 = −0.30. The code gets the offset back.

A measurement error in Δ becomes an error in d that is multiplied by 1 / (2 sin(θ/2)). At 86° (one cycle of our command) the factor is 0.73. At 180° it is 0.50, the best case. This is why larger turns give a better calibration, and why very small turns are rejected in the code.

A.4 Why far tags are less accurate

A simple camera model (the pinhole model) says that an object of real width s at distance d looks w pixels wide:

w = f · s / d   so   d = f · s / w

f is the focal length of the camera, in pixels. If the detector gets the width wrong by δw pixels, the distance is wrong by about:

δd ≈ ( d² / (f · s) ) · δw

Worked example. Use f = 500 px. This is an example value, not the real number for the Quest camera. The tag is s = 0.1651 m.
At 2 m: the tag is 41 px wide. One pixel of error gives δd ≈ 4 / 82.6 ≈ 0.05 m.
At 4 m: the tag is 21 px wide. One pixel of error gives δd ≈ 16 / 82.6 ≈ 0.19 m.
Two times the distance gives four times the error.

QuestNav uses the same d² rule to decide how much to trust a tag view. With n tags at an average distance d:

σ = 0.05 m × d² / n

3 tags at 2 m: σ ≈ 0.067 m (a strong pull). 2 tags at 5 m: σ ≈ 0.625 m (a gentle nudge). The height (z) gets twice this σ, because PnP finds height less well.

A.5 A Kalman filter in one dimension

A Kalman filter keeps two numbers for each value it tracks: the best guess x̂, and how unsure it is, the variance P (the standard deviation squared). It repeats two steps.

Predict (each VIO step): move the guess by the measured motion, and become a little less sure.

x̂ ← x̂ + ΔVIO     P ← P + Q

Update (each accepted tag view z, with variance R = σ²): blend the guess and the measurement.

K = P / (P + R)     x̂ ← x̂ + K·(z − x̂)     P ← (1 − K)·P

K is the gain, between 0 and 1. If the filter is unsure (P large) and the tag is good (R small), K is near 1 and the guess moves almost all the way to the tag. If the tag is poor, K is near 0 and the guess hardly moves.

Worked example. The filter thinks x̂ = 1.00 m with σ = 0.10 m, so P = 0.010. A tag view says z = 1.10 m.
3 tags at 2 m: σ = 0.067, R = 0.0045. K = 0.010 / 0.0145 = 0.69. New x̂ = 1.00 + 0.69 × 0.10 = 1.069 m. New P = 0.31 × 0.010 = 0.0031 (σ ≈ 0.056 m).
2 tags at 5 m: σ = 0.625, R = 0.39. K = 0.010 / 0.40 = 0.025. New x̂ = 1.0025 m. The far view hardly changes the guess.

QuestNav runs this with three values at once (x, y, z). Its documented process noise is Q = diag(0.02², 0.02², 0.01²) for each VIO step. Heading is not in the filter: it comes from VIO plus the heading offset that Phase 1 locked.

A.6 The robot's pose estimator

The pose estimator in the CTRE drivetrain uses the same idea with three values: x, y and θ. Wheel and gyro odometry is the "predict" step. Each addVisionMeasurement call is an "update" step, and the standard deviations you pass are the σ values for R.

Our QUESTNAV_STD_DEVS are (0.02 m, 0.02 m, 0.087 rad). An x/y σ of 0.02 m is small, so the gain is high and the estimate follows the headset closely. If the headset is not aligned to the field, the estimate follows it to the wrong place. That is another reason to confirm the alignment before a test.
